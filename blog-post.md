# What AI Is Actually Doing to the Cincinnati Job Market

*Ready to paste into a blog. The link at the bottom is the public one — no sign-in needed.*

---

Everyone has an opinion about what AI is doing to jobs. Almost nobody has measured it for a
specific place.

So I did, for Greater Cincinnati. I pulled the occupational data for all 1,101,030 jobs in the
metro, joined it to published AI exposure research, and checked what the region's largest
employers have actually said in their filings. Every figure traces to a public source, and the
whole analysis reproduces from scripts anyone can run.

Four findings, and the first one surprised me.

**Cincinnati's AI exposure is average. Boringly average.**

Weighting every occupation by how many people hold it, the metro scores 0.088 against a national
0.093. That gap is five thousandths of a standard deviation. The region is neither unusually
threatened nor unusually safe — claims in either direction aren't supported by its job mix.

**The real vulnerability isn't exposure. It's that nothing is growing.**

Cincinnati added 8,600 jobs between September 2024 and September 2026 — 0.7% across two years, on
a base of 1.18 million. Meanwhile the labour force shrank by 9,374. Looking forward, of 101,316
projected annual job openings, just 2,074 come from growth. The other 98% exist only because
someone leaves.

That changes what to worry about. In a growing economy, a technology that slows hiring reduces
the rate of expansion. In an economy with no growth cushion, it attacks the only door into
employment that exists.

**The region is buying AI capability, not building it.**

Cincinnati has 13% fewer computing jobs than its size implies. The biggest advertisers of AI roles
here are Deloitte, Kelly Services, PwC, Eliassen Group and Accenture — national consultancies and
staffing firms selling implementation capacity into the region. Among major local employers, only
GE Aerospace appears near the top. That makes capturing AI work an economic development question,
not a training question.

**Exposure rises with pay, which inverts the old automation story.**

Generative-AI exposure is negative across the bottom three wage quintiles and reaches +0.703
standard deviations in the top. The most exposed occupations here are accountants, HR specialists,
market research analysts and financial managers — not production workers. A worker earning $35,000
in food service faces very little measured exposure. One earning $75,000 in accounting faces a
great deal.

**What I won't claim**

That AI has caused measurable job losses in Cincinnati. That Procter & Gamble's 7,000-role
reduction is an AI story — the company attributes it to cost, tariffs and consumer uncertainty.
That banking's decline reflects AI: it fell 13.9% from 2019 but only 4.1% from 2024, so roughly
seven-tenths of it happened before generative AI existed.

Exposure measures task overlap. Whether overlap becomes job loss is exactly what remains
unestablished — and saying so is the point.

## Short version (LinkedIn, newsletter blurb)

I measured what AI is actually doing to the Greater Cincinnati job market, using government data
rather than predictions. Cincinnati's AI exposure turns out to be almost exactly average — 0.088
against a national 0.093. The real risk isn't exposure, it's that the region added just 0.7% more
jobs in two years, and 98% of projected openings come from workers leaving rather than growth. A
technology that slows hiring does far more damage in an economy with no growth cushion.
