## 1. Title

**Cincinnati AI Labor Market 2.1**
Early evidence on awareness, adoption, hiring and investment in Greater Cincinnati
[KetiB, AFFILIATION] · November 2026

*Notes.* This is the second published version of an independent study. The first asked how AI
is affecting the region's labor market and could not really answer, because it had nothing to
compare the region with. This version compares Cincinnati with other metros, and the tests
were written down before I looked at the data.

## 2. The question, and three kinds of evidence

- Is AI changing who gets hired here, and how far have people and firms taken it up?
- Infrastructure: data centers
- Employment signals: hiring, occupations, output, what employers say
- People and firms: public AI activity, and AI in company workflows

*Notes.* Three legs. Data centers are the physical investment. The employment data tell us
whether work is changing. The third leg is new in this version: are people showing up for AI,
and are firms putting it to work? I keep four things apart all the way through: people
convening around AI, what AI could do to a job, AI actually in use, and an employer saying AI
is why it cut jobs. Most arguments about AI and jobs mix those up.

## 3. What changed since version 1.0

- "Slow growth is the region's real fragility" — withdrawn
- "98% of openings are replacements" — true everywhere; it describes the method
- "The region buys AI rather than building it" — mostly industry mix
- "No measurable AI job losses" — now actually tested

*Notes.* I want to start with what I got wrong, or could not support. Version 1.0 said the
region's weakness was slow job growth. Against the Chamber's own peers, Cincinnati is 10th of
20. It said 98% of projected openings are just replacing people who leave. That is true of
every area I checked, the nation included. A reviewer raised most of these points, and the
reviewer was right. The full list is in the audit log.

## 4. Job growth is ordinary, not slow

*Chart: fig01_job_growth_peers.png*

- +0.7% in two years: the national rate
- Peer median: +0.2%. Seven peers lost jobs
- Rank: 10th of 20

*Notes.* Each bar is a metro, Cincinnati in blue. Growth of 0.7% over two years is not fast,
but it is the national rate and it is above the middle of the peer group. The labor force
story is also less clear than version 1.0 said: the Census survey shows it rising through
2025, and the fall in the monthly series is all in the last twelve months, in figures that
get revised in March.

## 5. Exposure to AI is average

- Just below the nation on all four measures
- Rises with pay in every metro examined: a feature of the technology, not of Cincinnati

*Notes.* I use four different measures of exposure, two that estimate what AI could do and two
built from records of what people actually use it for. Cincinnati is slightly below the nation
on all four and in the middle of its peers. The pattern that higher-paid jobs are more exposed
holds in all 33 metros I looked at, so it is not a local finding.

## 6. The central test: is the entry door narrowing?

- Compare new hires of 22-to-24-year-olds with 35-to-54-year-olds
- In AI-exposed industries against less-exposed ones
- 2023–25 against 2018–19
- Pass mark set in advance: a fall of 5% or more, beyond what chance produces

*Notes.* This is the heart of the study. Version 1.0 said the real risk from AI was fewer
entry-level hires in professional work, and that it would be hard to see. Census hiring
records let us see it. The idea is simple. If AI is replacing entry-level work, young people's
share of hiring should fall in the industries where AI can do the most, compared with
industries where it can do the least. I set the pass mark before running it.

## 7. Cincinnati does not show it. Most peers do.

*Chart: fig02_hiring_test.png*

- Cincinnati: −0.04 to +0.03, inside the chance range on all four measures
- Peer median: negative on all four
- Cincinnati ranks at or near the favorable end

*Notes.* Each grey dot is another metro and the blue dot is Cincinnati. Left of the dotted
line means young workers lost ground in AI-exposed industries. Most metros are to the left.
Cincinnati is at or near zero on every measure. So the pattern that national research has
found does appear across these metros, and Cincinnati is an exception to it. I checked whether
interest rates could explain the result by dropping finance and technology, and it did not
change.

## 8. Young workers' hiring fell everywhere

*Chart: fig03_hires_by_age.png*

- Ages 22–24: −9% in the most exposed industries, −16% in the middle, −11% in the least
- Ages 35–54: flat

*Notes.* This is the part not to miss. Young people's hiring did fall here. It fell by about
the same in industries where AI has little reach as in those where it has a lot. So the entry
door has narrowed in Cincinnati, for young workers in general. That points to something
economy-wide, a slower hiring market or fewer young people, and not to AI specifically.

## 9. Two employers, two paths

- Kroger: about 3,000 corporate cuts; the memo names technology, not AI
- Fifth Third: about 30% of new code AI-generated; no cuts attributed to AI
- Procter & Gamble: cuts attributed to costs and tariffs

*Notes.* Two of the region's largest employers use AI heavily. One is cutting corporate jobs
and one is not, and neither says AI is the reason. I apply one rule to every employer: does
its own statement name AI, name technology, or name neither? No verified company statement in
this study attributes a job cut to AI.

## 10. Are people showing up?

- CincyAI for Humans: 35 of 36 monthly meetings held in three years
- Stated membership: "over 1,700" (Oct 2025), "surpassed 2k" (March 2026) — the organizer's figures
- 17 of 18 events in the last year aimed beyond technologists

*Notes.* Nobody surveys how many people here use AI. What you can count is whether people
organize around it and turn up. The main community group has met every month for three years
and missed one meeting, for snow. Its membership figures are its own and are not audited, so I
quote them and do not add them to anything. Almost all its events are for a general audience.
This measures convening, not use.

## 11. Compared with peers: steady, but smaller

*Chart: fig06_awareness.png*

- Listed AI events per adult: 15th of 20
- Chapter meetings of a national builders' network: 10th of 20
- Search interest in AI tools: 17th of 20

*Notes.* AI events went up everywhere, so one city's count means little. These three measures
exist in the same form in every metro. Cincinnati is in the lower half on all three, though
inside the normal range on two. One caution: the middle chart counts a single national
organization, and Cincinnati's main group is home-grown, so that chart understates it.

## 12. Is awareness an early indicator?

- Across 20 metros: more AI events goes with more job postings naming AI tools (0.54)
- Suggestive, not settled: it does not hold on every measure
- Over time: could not be tested

*Notes.* I wanted to know whether community activity tells you anything about what firms do.
Across metros there is a link, but it is fragile, and twenty cities is a small sample. Whether
awareness comes first in time I could not test, because nobody publishes monthly AI postings
for the metro. So "early indicator" is a reasonable idea that this study has not proved.

## 13. Firms: assistants in production, agents mostly announced

- An AI system in production, on the record: 8 large employers
- An AI agent in production: 1, a local vendor
- No statement found: 10 large employers

*Notes.* An assistant drafts and suggests while a person does the work. An agent carries out a
task of several steps by itself. Companies use the word agent loosely, so I classified each
case by what the source actually describes, and I only counted a company's own record or a
named executive. Assistants are everywhere and at scale. Agents are mostly a plan. I set a
rule that I would only say agents are at work in the region's big employers with evidence from
three of them. I have one, and it is the company that sells them.

## 14. Adoption in the three states tracks the nation

*Chart: fig08_btos.png*

- Ohio 22%, Kentucky 23%, Indiana 20%, United States 24%
- No Cincinnati figure exists

*Notes.* About one business in four says it uses AI. The three states are close to the
national line. The Census Bureau publishes metro figures for the 25 largest metros and
Cincinnati is not one, so anyone quoting a Cincinnati adoption rate is not quoting this survey.

## 15. Capability: the gap is in research, not training

*Chart: fig05_capability.png*

- AI patents: 16th of 20. University computing research: 14th. Computing graduates: 8th
- 58% of the shortfall in computing jobs is industry mix

*Notes.* The region has fewer computing jobs than its size would suggest. Most of that is
because of which industries are here. On building capability, the picture is split: the region
trains computing graduates at an above-average rate and produces AI patents and research at a
below-average one. Where those graduates go to work is the next thing worth finding out.

## 16. Data centers: investment, few permanent jobs

*Chart: fig07_datacenter_load.png*

- 250 MW under contract with the utility; a further 1,080 MW is not
- One public commitment: 120 jobs for 250 MW
- Industry jobs in the published counties: +401 in three years

*Notes.* The firmest number is from Duke Energy's own filing to the grid operator in
September: 250 megawatts committed. The figure of 565 megawatts that circulates adds planned
facilities to existing ones. On jobs, I set three tests that would have overturned my earlier
finding, and none is met. The largest project has promised 120 jobs. Data centers bring
investment and tax base. They are not a jobs program.

## 17. Two readings, and what to watch

- Bull: no entry-door effect; assistants adopted without attributed job losses; a steady civic community
- Bear: later, not better — lower-half on events, postings, patents, research, productivity growth
- Watch: the hiring test each quarter; young workers' hires; agents moving from announced to running

*Notes.* The same evidence supports two stories. The hopeful one is that AI here is
complementing work, not replacing it. The cautious one is that Cincinnati looks fine because
it is behind, and the effect seen elsewhere has yet to arrive. I do not know which is right.
The monitoring sheet lists fourteen indicators and how to update each, most of them every
quarter or every six months.

## 18. What this study cannot tell you, and how it is checked

- Not done: state layoff notices; which employers attend AI events; training on offer
- Cannot be known from public data: a Cincinnati AI-use rate; AI's causal effect on any job
- 40 regression tests; 21 documented departures from the plan; everything reproducible

*Notes.* I would rather tell you what is missing than have you find it. Four pieces of the
plan were not completed and the report says where each would have gone. Every number in these
slides is produced by a script and checked by a test, and where I departed from the plan I
wrote down why. The code, the data and the pre-registration are all public.
