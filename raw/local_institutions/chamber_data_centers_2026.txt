Executive Summary I Data Centers: A Cincinnati Regional Perspective

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Data centers as critical IT infrastructure have existed for decades. The Cincinnati region is
home to multiple facilities which are more than ten years old. However, the prevalence,
scale, and purpose of data centers has evolved over the years.

There are an estimated 19 data centers operating or planned in the
Cincinnati metropolitan area with an estimated total capacity of 565
megawatts (MW).

The state of Ohio is one of the world's largest data center markets - and growing.
In Central Ohio, the world's first gigawatt-capable data center is currently underway as
part of a Meta campus expansion, and reportedly the largest AI data center in the world
was announced in Pike County about 100 miles from Cincinnati.

Data center growth represents opportunities for the Cincinnati metro area: capital
investment, infrastructure upgrades, jobs, tax revenue, and corporate partnerships. There
are also proven challenges relative to utility demand, environmental factors, and land
opportunity cost. "Data Centers: A Cincinnati Regional Perspective" offers a localized lens
to a global wave of data center investment to inform local dialogue and decision-making.

Below are several examples of localities that have worked alongside data center
developers to benefit their communities.

Note: Policy and regulatory frameworks differ in these states and don't always mirror the
landscape in Ohio and Kentucky. These case studies illustrate how other states are working within
their own policy frameworks to structure deals in ways that best serve them.

PROPERTY TAX RELIEF CASE STUDY
Columbia County, GA -- Google is building two data centers in Columbia County, Georgia,
and county officials have been working on crafting a plan to ensure the community
benefits. Prior to finalizing the development agreement, the Columbia County Board
of Commissioners and the Columbia County Chamber of Commerce worked with the
community and data center developers to set priorities by hosting public meetings and
in-person visits with nearby property owners to hear concerns around taxes, quality of life,
and utility costs.1 In August 2026, Columbia County's Economic Development Authority
approved a memorandum of understanding with Kinetic Infrastructure Solutions, a
subsidiary of Alphabet Inc., that includes a financial commitment of $17 billion.2 County
Commission Chairman Doug Duncan said the goal of attracting data centers is for a
portion of the project's revenue to offset resident property taxes; property taxes currently
make up 40% of the county's total budget.3 Achieving this required a change in state law,
and in May 2026, Georgia enacted House Bill 439, enabling counties to fund property tax
relief using data-center revenue.4 The measure must still clear a local referendum, which
Columbia County voters will decide in November 2026. Google separately committed to
cover 100% of the project's energy costs.5

Loudoun County, Culpeper County, and Prince William County, VA -- A 2026 report
prepared by Mangum Economics for the Northern Virgina Technology Council modeled
what residential property tax rates would need to be to offset the loss of data center
tax revenue and fund county operations.6 Without that revenue, Culpeper's rate would
need to rise 34%, Prince William's 29%, and Loudoun's would need to nearly double
(+91%), adding roughly ~$5,800/year to the median homeowner's property tax bill in
Loudoun County.7 Regionally, the data center industry paid an estimated $1.3 billion in
direct property taxes in 2024, supporting more than $2 billion in total local government
revenue.8 This is an example of the value data centers and the tax revenue they generate
can bring in and help offset what would otherwise be a larger tax burden on residents.

DATA CENTERS in Action

Property tax relief takeaway: Columbia County and Northern Virginia show two ways
data center revenue can directly reduce what residents pay in property taxes, rather than
just offsetting any new costs those projects create. Columbia County had to change state
law first, and the plan still needs voter approval. This is proof that this kind of relief usually
takes both legislative groundwork and local buy-in, not just a deal between a county and
a developer. Northern Virginia's numbers show the flip side: what residents would lose if
that revenue disappeared, with some counties facing property tax increases that would
nearly double homeowners' bills. Both showcase that data center tax revenue is large
enough to meaningfully lower what residents pay. Capturing that value takes deliberate
policy design. Localities could instead balance the budget through spending cuts, but
that approach comes with its own tradeoffs. That's why our framework calls for directing a
defined share of data center revenue toward real, visible relief for existing residents.

EDUCATION FUNDING CASE STUDY

Marysville, OH -- Marysville, a Columbus-area suburb, has used tax abatement
agreements paired with Payment in Lieu of Taxes (PILOT) provisions to preserve
education funding from major data center investment while remaining competitive for
site selection. 9 Marysville City Council approved a 15-year, 100% property tax abatement
on new construction for a $1 billion, roughly 600-acre data center project.10 In exchange,
the developer agreed to make PILOT payments to the Marysville Exempted Village
School District, the City of Marysville, and the Ohio Hi-Point Career Center, structured so
local taxing entities still receive approximately 50% of the revenue that could have been
collected absent the abatement.11 Land underlying the project remains fully taxable
throughout the abatement period, with land taxes alone projected to generate roughly
$18 million over 15 years for the schools and city. 12 The agreement also includes a revenue-
sharing trigger, once the project's payroll surpasses $2 million, the city splits the resulting
income tax revenue with the school district. 13 A separate AWS data center campus nearby
(also in Marysville) illustrates an even more direct approach, in this agreement AWS
committed to $15 million in annual payments to Marysville Schools over 15 years, alongside
a 30-year, 100% property tax exemption on the assessed value of the data centers.14
Together, these two agreements show how municipalities can structure abatements to
preserve, and in some cases provide dedicated, school funding even while offering the tax
incentives development requires, offsetting concerns that data centers erode the local tax
base that schools depend on.

Education funding takeaway: Marysville and Sidney each illustrate a distinct approach
to how localities can protect education funding while pursuing data center investment.
Their locally negotiated agreements use tools such as PILOT payments, taxable carve-
outs, and income tax-sharing arrangements. Neither approach is a direct template for our
region. Rather, they illustrate how municipalities and affected stakeholders can develop
solutions that reflect local circumstances and priorities.

Their experiences reinforce two principles in our framework: fostering direct and open
collaboration between data centers and host communities and capturing the fiscal
benefits data centers bring to those communities. By engaging affected school districts
and other local stakeholders as part of the development process, communities can
negotiate arrangements that reflect local priorities and preserve meaningful fiscal
benefits from data center investment, while maintaining flexibility over the economic
development tools and abatement structures they choose to use.

Paducah, Kentucky -- A parallel project from the Department of Energy is underway at
the former Paducah Gaseous Diffusion Plant, another Cold War-era uranium enrichment
site in cleanup since operations ended in 2013.28 The development is entirely privately
funded, representing roughly $100 billion in investment and expected to create
approximately 8,000 construction jobs and 600 permanent jobs. 29 The plan pairs a large
AI data center campus with new natural gas generation and battery storage to meet
the electricity demands, with Brookfield Asset Management developing and operating
the campus and NextEra Energy building the power infrastructure. 30Officials have
described it as following the same redevelopment model set at Piketon, with initial phase
construction possible within a few years and full build-out targeted for the early 2030s.31

Site-selection takeaway: Piketon and Paducah illustrate a distinct version of "adaptive
reuse", pairing hyperscale data center demand with large, federally owned industrial sites
that already have substantial power infrastructure, transmission access, and a built-in
economic-redevelopment case, rather than greenfield or in-community sites. These sites
show where data centers are a clear opportunity for the states: brownfield and legacy
industrial land with power and infrastructure already in place. Where sites like these exist,
Ohio and Kentucky should work proactively to seize opportunities.

Cincinnati has no obvious comparable federal legacy site, and this model may not
translate to urban areas, so it isn't a direct template for our region. It is a useful contrast:
site characteristics like power capacity, existing infrastructure, remediation status, and
community economic need clearly drive where developers land. That reinforces why our
framework's site-selection principle - weighing the opportunity cost of land, prioritizing
adaptive reuse, and right-sizing development to fit community character - matters for the
sites Cincinnati will actually have under consideration.

Commonwealth of Kentucky

In 2024, the Kentucky General Assembly passed House Bill 8, creating a sale and use tax
exemption for qualified data center projects covering the purchase of equipment, which
can last up to 50 years for projects meeting the highest investment tier. The original
exemption was narrowly targeted and applied only to consolidated local governments
with a population of at least 500,000, in practice, just Louisville/Jefferson County, and
required a minimum capital investment of $450 million or $150 million for the project
organizer.35

The exemption is administered by Kentucky's Cabinet for Economic Development
and covers data center equipment and software broadly including servers, routers,
and systems related to electricity, cooling, water usage, and security. It excludes site
development, shell building construction, electricity purchases, and office equipment

Ohio Chamber of Commerce

The Ohio Chamber of Commerce, through its research arm the Ohio Chamber
Foundation, has taken a strongly pro-growth position on data centers, releasing an
"Economic Impact Study of Data Centers in Ohio" that frames the industry as a core driver
of the state's economic future. The Chamber argues that Ohio's more than $40 billion in
private data center investment is reshaping the state's economy beyond its traditional
manufacturing and agriculture base, elevating Columbus into the top tier of North
American data center hubs and positioning Ohio as an aspiring "Silicon Heartland."

The study's headline findings anchor the Chamber's advocacy: in 2024 alone, data center
activity supported over 95,000 jobs and generated $26.4 billion in total economic output
statewide, with projections showing the industry growing to support roughly 132,500
jobs and contribute nearly $20.2 billion annually to state GDP by 2030. The Chamber also
points to the state's sales tax exemption program, Ohio's primary data center incentive, as
a fiscal success, citing a 2.1-to-1 return on investment in the study's modeling.

Rather than treating the incentive debate as settled, the Chamber frames its findings as
the basis for a forward-looking policy agenda. It calls for incentive agreements to continue
emphasizing local benefits, workforce development, and transparent performance
tracking; for cross-sector collaboration to address the mismatch between rapid data
center construction timelines (18­36 months) and much slower electric grid upgrade
timelines (5­10 years), including bridge strategies like natural gas generation, expanded
behind-the-meter power, and fast-tracked approval for sites like brownfields; and for
sustained investment in workforce training tied to the industry's technical needs.38

DEFINITIONS

Capacity: Generally measured in megawatt (MW), describes the size of a data center
based on the electrical power needed to operate
Cincinnati Region: Refers to the 15-county tri-state metropolitan statistical area (MSA) as
defined by the U.S. Census Bureau
Economic Impact: Jobs, earnings, and local taxes were calculated with the Lightcast
economic impact model. Lightcast uses Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), Bureau of
Labor Statistics (BLS), and Census data to estimate these variables. Rather than relying
solely on the model's industry assumptions by NAICS code, we customized the spending
input to account for increased investment from the utility industry. The components of an
economic impact study include:

   Direct: economic activity being modeled (addition of new jobs, additional spending,
   etc.)
   Indirect: the impact of direct activity on the supply chain (the ripple effect through
   industries)
   Induced: broader impact of new household spending from wage-earners of the direct
   and indirect effects
   Multiplier: "a way of measuring how important one industry is to other industries in
   the region. So if an industry has a multiplier of 2.5, for every positive or negative change
   on that industry, the total effect on the regional economy will be 2.5 times the original
   change." (source: Lightcast)
Facility Types:
   Co-location: data centers operated by an owner who rents shared space to multiple
   tenants
   Enterprise: data centers which handle a single company's own data
   Hyperscale: data centers designed to handle massive computing needs and often
   associated with large technology companies such as AWS, Google, Microsoft, etc.
