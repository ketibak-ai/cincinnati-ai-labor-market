                             01      & MIGRATION

                                     The Cincinnati region entered 2025 continuing its growth
                                     trajectory. In the past year, the region welcomed 13,000 new
                                     residents, and since 2020, nearly 61,000 people have chosen
                                     to call the region home. Growth here isn't concentrated in one
                                     place, either. No single county accounted for more than 20%
                                     of the region's total population gains year-over-year. Clermont
                                     County saw the largest gains, accounting for one-fifth of the
                                     region's growth this year, despite making up just 9% of the
                                     region's population.

                                     Looking at our peers, a different story emerges. Southern metro
                                     areas like Austin, Atlanta, Charlotte, and Tampa are growing at
                                     more than double the rate of Cincinnati, mirroring trends in
                                     both job growth and the young professional population-- an
                                     indicator that population growth and economic strength tend to
                                     rise and fall together. High-growth areas continue to experience
                                     large population changes from both domestic and international
                                     migration. The vast majority of Cincinnati's growth comes from
                                     international migration, underscoring the need to continue to
                                     focus on both attracting and retaining domestic and international
                                     talent in order to grow and thrive as a region.

POPULATION CHANGE

growth of 2.7% since 2020. Cincinnati is the 30th largest metro in the country and
the most populous metro area that includes Ohio or Kentucky. The region's growth
closely tracks that of the nation and its total population, and population growth
lands in the mid-range of its peers.

                             02       ECONOMY

                                      Cincinnati's regional economy is strong, resilient, and growing.
                                      At $162 billion, the Cincinnati region's Regional Real Gross
                                      Domestic Product (GDP) is the largest in both Ohio and
                                      Kentucky. That strength is reflected in Cincinnati's global reach:
                                      exports remain a major contributor to the economy, and the region
                                      boasts the highest export value per capita among peer metros.

                                      Economic strength is also translating into job growth, most
                                      noticeably in Northern Kentucky. Boone County saw the largest
                                      percent job growth of all Cincinnati metro area counties from
                                      2015 to 2025 and the ZIP code for Florence, KY had the largest job
                                      growth in the past year - adding over 1,600 from 2024 to 2025.

                                      Underlying this activity is a strong corporate presence and
                                      diversity in industry leadership. The region's eight Fortune 500
                                      headquartered companies anchor a deep corporate base, driving
                                      employment both directly and through their cascading impact.

                                      Despite positive momentum, growth has been measured.
                                      Cincinnati's economy is performing better than its three states over
                                      time, but growing slower than the country and southern peers.
                                      Job growth is also more modest than in competing regions and
                                      the U.S. overall, highlighting the need to sustain and accelerate the
                                      region's economic capacity.

         COMMUNITY
         & HEALTH

         With a regional lens, examining community and health factors
         paints a picture of relative stability. The region's median household
         income ­ $81,489 ­ places it right in the middle among peers and
         higher than our Ohio peers, even when accounting for cost of living
         differences. Child poverty has decreased by 4.8 percentage points in
         the past ten years, the food insecurity rate is a lower rate than over
         half our peers, and 90% of City of Cincinnati residents have access
         to a park within a 10-minute walk of their homes. Higher education
         completions present greater strengths for the region, with steady
         rates of local degrees and certificates granted.

         Regional figures present promising landscapes, but examining these
         factors at a more granular level or against demographic delineations
         indicates significant disparities. The regional racial income gap
         is $36,339 between the median Non-Hispanic White household
         and median Black household. On a county-to-county basis, there
         is a $48,606 gap between Warren County, Ohio, and Bracken
         County, Kentucky. Food insecurity presents similar challenges
         with over 127,000 people in Hamilton County alone experiencing
         food insecurity. Disparities, both intraregional and against peers,
         also persist in end-of-life metrics including infant mortality and
         life expectancy, highlighting the need for sustained community
         commitment to better outcomes.

topography, land use, human activity, and weather.

                                      The Cincinnati region's housing and transportation landscape
                                      reflects pressing challenges alongside reasons for optimism.
                                      Housing is becoming less affordable, and renters face particularly
                                      significant cost pressures with nearly 25% of the region's renters
                                      paying 50% or more of their income toward housing. Housing supply
                                      gains are minimal relative to other peers and when measured against
                                      population growth. Rising home values alongside the disparity in
                                      homeownership indicate rising cost burdens and unequal access to
                                      wealth generation through home ownership.

                                      Similar to most of the U.S., the majority of residents commute by
                                      personal vehicle. Despite public transportation's relatively small share of
                                      commuters, Cincinnati Metro, our region's largest public transit provider,
                                      experienced remarkable ridership growth in the last 5 years, ranking
                                      third among peers for ridership growth and increasing in ridership
                                      by 80% since 2020. TANK in Northern Kentucky has also experienced
                                      growth, with ridership increasing 22% over the same period.

                                      Alternative modes of transportation are expected to expand further
                                      with Metro Rapid, the planned bus rapid transit system along major
                                      corridors, and increased trail development. Major infrastructure projects
                                      like the Brent Spence Bridge Project and Western Hills viaduct are also
                                      beginning construction. These investments create an opportunity to
                                      better connect the region, and in tandem with housing strategies, can
                                      improve affordability and quality of life in the region.
